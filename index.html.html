How to sell company data to AI starts with an inventory, not an export

Before you take a single call from a data buyer, build a one-page inventory. It is the cheapest way to learn what you have and what buyers will look for.

By Noah Loul, Founder and CEO, SourceX

To sell company data to AI, start with a one-page inventory: pick one workflow, list its source systems, describe record types, date ranges and volumes without exporting anything, flag personal and third-party data, note which outcomes are recorded, and assign an owner to each rights question. Buyers quote wildly different prices, so the inventory is your best negotiating tool.

How to sell company data to AI starts with an inventory, not an export

If you are wondering how to sell company data to AI, the first step has nothing to do with buyers. It is a one-page document that describes what you have, written by people who know your systems. You build it before anyone exports a single file.

The market is real and moving fast. MIT Technology Review reported on October 13, 2026 that Google bought Spirit Airlines’ data for $10 million, and that SimpleClosure, a startup wind-down platform, now sees code or data sales in 47% of its deals. It also reported that pricing is opaque and buyers quote wildly different numbers. One broker, Sunset, values data by headcount, years in operation, data volume and how the team used its tools.

That last point is your clue. The factors buyers care about are things you can describe from an admin console in an afternoon. An inventory turns a vague “we have lots of data” into a document you can put in front of three buyers and compare their answers.

Steps 1 and 2: pick one workflow and list its source systems

Do not try to inventory the whole company. Pick one workflow that runs end to end and repeats thousands of times: customer support, sales from lead to close, software development from ticket to release, or project delivery. AI developers building agents want to see how real work moves from a request to a result, so a single complete workflow is more useful than a pile of disconnected folders.

Then list every system that touches that workflow. For support, that might be the help desk (Zendesk or similar), the CRM, the internal chat tool and the knowledge base. For engineering, it might be Jira, GitHub and Slack. Write down the system name, who administers it and whether you still have access. Wound-down companies often discover a key system was switched off. Note that too.

Step 3: describe records, date ranges and volumes without exporting

For each system, describe what is inside in plain words. What is a single record? A ticket with its full conversation thread? A deal with its notes and stage history? A pull request with its review comments? Then add the date range and an approximate count.

You can get almost all of this from built-in reports or admin settings. Ticket counts by year, number of active users, repository count, storage used. Round numbers are fine. “About 400,000 tickets, 2018 to 2025” is exactly the level of detail a buyer needs at this stage.

Resist the urge to send samples. Raw records contain names, emails and account details. Nothing should leave your systems until there is a signed agreement and an agreed plan for de-identification.

Steps 4 and 5: flag people data, third-party content and outcomes

Next, mark the sensitive layers. These do not disqualify a dataset, but they shape how it can be prepared and who must sign off.

  • Employee data: HR records, performance notes, internal messages. Often the most sensitive layer, and frequently excluded.
  • Customer personal data: names, emails, phone numbers, addresses, payment details inside tickets or CRM notes.
  • Third-party content: vendor proposals, files customers uploaded, material under NDA or a separate license.
  • Contractor work: deliverables whose rights may sit with the contractor unless their agreement assigned them.
  • Outcomes: does each record show how things ended? Resolved and confirmed, deal won or lost, ticket shipped, refund issued.

Step 6: give every rights question an owner

Each flag from the last step becomes a question, and every question needs a named person. Who confirms the company has the right to license help desk content under its customer terms? Your general counsel or outside lawyer. Who confirms contractor agreements assigned rights? Whoever holds those contracts. Who decides whether employee chat is in or out? Usually the CEO with HR and counsel.

Write the question, the owner and a target date in one row each. Open questions are normal. A buyer would much rather see “customer terms under review, owner: GC, due March 15” than a blanket promise that everything is fine.

This is also where you learn how a buyer works. Some licensing companies, SourceX among them, start with a short no-upload fit check and let the company approve the final scope, which means your inventory is something you answer from, not something you hand over.

Your one-page inventory includes
  • ☐ Source systems and their owners
  • ☐ Record types, dates, volumes
  • ☐ People and third-party data flags
  • ☐ Rights questions with owners

Step 7: produce the one-page inventory (a worked example)

Pull it together on a single page. Here is how it might look for a hypothetical 300-person home services company that has run its support workflow for seven years. Every number below is invented for illustration.

  • Help desk (Zendesk): about 600,000 tickets, 2018 to 2025, full threads; customer names and addresses present; resolution status plus reopen flags recorded. Owner: Head of Support.
  • CRM (Salesforce): about 90,000 accounts with service history, 2018 to 2025; customer personal data present. Owner: VP Sales.
  • Knowledge base (internal wiki): about 1,200 articles, 2019 to 2025; company-authored, low sensitivity. Owner: Support Ops.
  • Team chat (Slack): about 3 million messages; employee personal data present; recommended for exclusion pending HR review. Owner: CEO.
  • Open rights questions: customer terms (GC, 30 days), technician contractor agreements (COO, 30 days).

Frequently asked questions

What kind of company data do AI companies buy?

Mostly records of real work. Support conversations with problems and resolutions, business workflows with decisions and outcomes, sales and CRM history, software engineering data such as code and tickets, documents and knowledge bases, call recordings and contracts. Data that shows how a task ended is usually more useful than loose files.

Do I have to upload data to find out if it is worth anything?

No. You can describe systems, record types, date ranges and volumes from admin reports without exporting anything. Many buyers can give an initial view of fit from that description alone. Avoid sending raw samples before an agreement, because they almost always contain personal information.

Can a company that has shut down still sell its data?

Yes, if someone still controls the assets and the systems or backups still exist. MIT Technology Review reported that SimpleClosure, a wind-down platform, includes code or data sales in 47% of its deals. Check who holds authority after the shutdown before contacting buyers.

Who should build the company’s data inventory?

A small team: one executive sponsor who can approve licensing, the administrators of each source system, and your lawyer for the rights questions. The administrators supply counts and date ranges, the lawyer owns the rights rows, and the sponsor decides what stays in or out.

What makes business data valuable for AI training?

Bigger is not always better. Buyers tend to look for data that shows real work from start to finish.

Complete workflows beat loose files

A support ticket with its full thread, resolution and follow-up teaches a model far more than a folder of unrelated attachments. AI teams building agents want to see how a request becomes a result.

Recorded outcomes

Records that show how things ended, such as a deal won or lost, a refund issued or a bug shipped, give developers a signal for what “good” looks like.

Clean provenance

Buyers need confidence that you hold the rights to license the data. Clear ownership, documented customer terms and contractor assignments reduce friction and speed up decisions.

Consistency over time

A steady format across many years is easier to prepare than several migrations of different tools. Note any system changes in your inventory.

De-identifying company data before licensing: a practical overview

Raw business records almost always contain personal details. De-identification is the step that makes sharing responsible.

Know where personal data hides

Names and emails are obvious, but addresses, phone numbers and account IDs often sit inside free-text notes, call transcripts and attachments.

Combine automated and human review

Pattern-based tools catch many identifiers, while a human review of samples inside your own environment catches what the tools miss.

Agree the plan before data moves

Put the method, the reviewers and the sign-off process in writing before any export. Keep copies inside your systems until the agreement is signed.

Involve counsel

Requirements differ by region and by industry. Your lawyer should confirm what de-identification must achieve for your data. This article is general information, not legal advice.

Five questions to ask any AI data buyer before you share anything

Buyers differ widely in how they work. These questions help you compare them fairly.

1. Do I need to upload anything to get a first view?

A good starting point is a description-only fit check based on systems, record types, dates and volumes.

2. Who approves the final scope?

You should be able to decide what is included or excluded, especially for employee and customer data.

3. How will personal data be handled?

Ask for the de-identification method and who performs it.

4. How is pricing determined?

Pricing is opaque in this market. Ask what factors drive the quote so you can compare offers side by side.

5. What happens to the data afterward?

Clarify permitted uses, retention, and what your contract says about deletion.
